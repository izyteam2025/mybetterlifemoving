Learning how to choose a moving company comes down to a handful of checks that take less time than reading reviews. You have probably opened a few tabs, collected two quotes that don't match, and wondered whether the cheaper one will even turn up. That concern makes sense: moving is one of the few purchases where you hand strangers everything you own. This guide covers what to verify before you hire, how to tell a carrier from a broker, how to compare quotes and coverage, the warning signs worth walking away over, and when to book so you have room to check properly.

## Key Takeaways

- Check registration first: a Massachusetts DPU license for in-state moves, and an FMCSA USDOT number for moves across state lines.
- Ask whether the company is a carrier with its own crews and trucks or a broker that passes your job to someone else.
- Compare written quotes on what they include, not just the total, and ask how the price can change.
- Basic coverage on interstate moves pays no more than 60 cents per pound per item, so ask about other options.
- Book four to eight weeks ahead, and earlier for summer and month-end dates.

## What to Check Before You Hire Movers

Start with the paperwork, because it is the fastest filter.

- Registration. In Massachusetts, [household moving companies must be licensed by the Department of Public Utilities](https://www.mass.gov/guides/moving-within-massachusetts). In North Carolina, the NC Department of Justice tells consumers to [confirm the mover's NC Utilities Commission certificate](https://ncdoj.gov/moving) for in-state moves. Any interstate move needs FMCSA registration with a USDOT number.
- A real address and a consistent name across the website, the quote, the contract and the truck.
- A written estimate, made after someone has seen your belongings in person or on a video call.
- Plain answers about what changes the price.

## Mover or Broker: Know Who Shows Up

A carrier owns its trucks and employs its crews. A broker takes your booking and arranges the move through a separate carrier, often one you never vetted. Brokers aren't automatically bad, but you should know which you are hiring and who will be in your home.

Ask three direct questions: Who will be on the truck? Whose name is on the truck? Whose name is on the contract? If the answers differ, find out why before you sign. Better Life Moving is a carrier, so the crew that quotes your move is from the company that shows up.

## How to Compare Moving Quotes

Get at least two or three written quotes, then compare what each includes rather than only the bottom line. Packing, materials, stairs, disassembly and travel time are where quotes differ most.

### Know What Kind of Price You Are Looking At

Local moves are commonly billed by the hour. With us, local moves are billed hourly, with travel time from our yard added, and blankets, shrink wrap, tape and truck costs (gas, mileage, tolls) are included in the hourly rate. For interstate moves, federal rules describe two kinds of estimate. A binding one fixes the price for the listed items. A non-binding one is a projection, and under federal regulations [the mover cannot make you pay more than 110 percent of it at delivery](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/appendix-A_to_part_375). Our [long distance moving service](/long-distance-movers/) quotes those moves at a flat rate built from your inventory.

### Read the Coverage Line

Coverage is often where a cheap quote saves money. For interstate shipments, the FMCSA explains that under released value protection [the mover is liable for no more than 60 cents per pound per article](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move/valuation-insurance). A heavy, inexpensive dresser is reasonably covered at that rate. A light, valuable laptop is not. Ask what full value protection costs.

### Match the Company to the Job

A piano, a gun safe or a collection of artwork needs equipment and training that a standard crew may not have. Ask how often the company handles items like yours. We send specialist crews for [moving a piano](/piano-movers/) and for [white glove handling of art and antiques](/white-glove-movers/).

## Red Flags and When to Book

Walk away from:

- A quote far below every other one, given without seeing your home.
- A demand for a large deposit, especially in cash, by wire or through a payment app.
- No written contract, or one with blank delivery dates.
- A phone answered only with "movers", with no company name.
- Pressure to sign today.

Book four to eight weeks out, and earlier for summer, weekends and month-end dates. If your timeline is tight, shorten the checking rather than skip it. Our [short-notice moving crews](/last-minute-movers/) can help whenever availability allows.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How Do I Choose the Best Moving Company Near Me?

Choose a moving company by checking its registration for your type of move, confirming a real address and consistent company name, and getting a written quote after it has seen your belongings. Then ask whether it is a carrier or a broker, compare coverage options, and read reviews spread over several years rather than one recent burst.

### Are Cheap Movers Always a Bad Idea?

Not always, but a quote far below every other one deserves questions. Ask what it includes, how it was calculated and how it can change on moving day. Some low quotes leave out packing, stairs or travel time, and some assume the most basic coverage. Compare the same scope of work across quotes before deciding.

### How Much Should I Pay as a Deposit to Movers?

Ideally little or nothing. A small deposit to reserve a date can be normal, and some reputable movers take none. Be wary of any company that wants a large share of the price before your belongings are moved, particularly in cash, by wire transfer or through a payment app that offers no way to dispute the charge.

### What Coverage Do Movers Provide for Damaged Items?

On interstate moves, movers must offer full value protection, and the basic alternative, released value protection, pays no more than 60 cents per pound per article. Ask each mover which options it offers for your move, what each costs, and how to file a claim. Check whether your own renter's or homeowner's policy covers goods in transit.

### What Should I Do If Movers Damage or Lose Something?

Photograph your valuables before the move and keep a written inventory. If something is damaged or missing, note it at delivery, then file a written claim with the company as soon as you can. For unresolved interstate disputes, you can file a complaint with the FMCSA; for in-state moves, contact your state's regulator.
